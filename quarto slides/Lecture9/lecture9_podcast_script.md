MIGUEL: MM II.

## A. The interest tax deduction

MEI: In most countries interest counts as an expense, so it's deductible, fully or partially. Dividends are paid from after-tax profits. The slide gives the 2026 rules: France, marginal corporate rate twenty-five percent, interest deductible up to the higher of three million euros or thirty percent of EBITDA. The US, federal rate twenty-one percent, deductible up to thirty percent of EBIT.

MIGUEL: Thirty percent of EBITDA. So the deduction has a ceiling in real life.

MEI: It has a ceiling, and keep that ceiling in your pocket, because it comes back in section E as one reason firms don't borrow without limit. For simplicity, the lecture assumes interest is fully deductible. In words: when the firm pays interest, its taxable profit falls, so the government pays part of the interest bill. When it pays dividends, the government pays nothing.

MIGUEL: The example.

MEI: A company with no leverage, profit of ten, tax rate thirty-four percent. The CEO borrows eighty at five percent and reduces equity by the same amount. The balance sheet goes from one hundred of equity to twenty of equity and eighty of debt. Same assets, same profit of ten before interest and tax. Six steps.

MIGUEL: Go.

MEI: Step one, the interest: eighty times five percent, four, to the lenders. Step two, taxable profit: ten minus four, six, instead of ten. Step three, the tax: thirty-four percent of six, two point zero four, instead of three point four. Step four, the shareholders: six minus two point zero four, three point nine six, instead of six point six. Step five, all investors together: three point nine six plus four, seven point nine six, instead of six point six. Step six, the gap: seven point nine six minus six point six, one point three six. Which is exactly the tax saved: three point four minus two point zero four, and also thirty-four percent of four.

MIGUEL: So net income falls, and investors get more.

MEI: That's the box, and it's the sentence that confuses people. Net income falls, from six point six to three point nine six, but investors as a group receive more, seven point nine six against six point six. The difference, one point three six, is tax the firm no longer pays: the interest tax shield.

MIGUEL: So if I only looked at the income statement, I'd think the CEO destroyed value. Net income down forty percent.

MEI: And that's the trap. The income statement is written from the shareholders' seat, after interest. From the seat of all investors together, shareholders plus lenders, the firm pays out more than before. Three parties share the EBIT: lenders, shareholders and the government. Debt moves money from the third party to the first two. The professor put it in six numbered steps precisely so you can see at which line the government's share shrinks: step three.

MIGUEL: Step three, the tax line.

MEI: Step three. The first summary: interest tax shield equals the corporate tax rate times the interest payments. Cash flows to investors with leverage equal cash flows without leverage plus the tax shield. The pie, EBIT, is the same, but the government's slice shrinks, so more is left for investors.

MIGUEL: And the figure with a thousand of EBIT.

MEI: Same story at forty percent tax. Unlevered: tax four hundred, shareholders six hundred. Levered with five hundred of interest: tax two hundred, shareholders three hundred. Investors get five hundred plus three hundred, eight hundred: two hundred more, which is forty percent of five hundred.

## B. MM2: debt, tax and firm value

MEI: Section B. MM2 Proposition 1, in the box: the total value of the levered firm exceeds the value of the firm without leverage by the present value of the tax savings from debt. V levered equals V unlevered plus the present value of the interest tax shield. Debt adds value only through the tax it saves.

MIGUEL: And the present value?

MEI: Under one hypothesis: the firm keeps a constant, perpetual debt at the risk-free rate. Then the annual tax shield is the tax rate times the interest, which is the tax rate times r times D. In the example: thirty-four percent of five percent of eighty, one point three six every year, for ever. What is that worth today? Slide 25 writes the infinite sum, and this is one place where we skip the algebra: a constant payment every year for ever is worth the payment divided by the rate. So the tax rate times r times D, divided by r, is the tax rate times D. The r cancels.

MIGUEL: So the shield is worth tax rate times debt. That's it? Why does the interest rate disappear?

MEI: Because it appears twice and cancels. A higher rate means a bigger saving each year, the tax rate times a bigger interest bill. But a higher rate also means each future saving is discounted more heavily. The two effects offset exactly, and what's left is the tax rate times the amount borrowed. It's a lovely result: you don't need to know the interest rate to value a permanent tax shield.

MIGUEL: As long as.

MEI: As long as the shield has the same risk as the debt, which is the hypothesis of a permanent, constant debt. MM2 Proposition 1 becomes V levered equals V unlevered plus the tax rate times D. In the example: thirty-four percent of eighty, twenty-seven point two. Each euro of permanent debt adds thirty-four cents of value.

MIGUEL: The simple example.

MEI: Firm U is financed with five hundred of equity, no leverage, tax rate thirty percent. Firm L is identical but has two hundred and fifty of debt. Step one, U is worth five hundred. Step two, the shield: thirty percent of two fifty, seventy-five. Step three: L is worth five seventy-five. The graph: V unlevered is a flat line, the business is the same whatever the debt; V levered rises in a straight line with slope zero point three. And the box with the warning: taken literally, MM2 Proposition 1 says borrow as much as possible.

MIGUEL: Which is suspicious.

MEI: Which is why there's a section E.

## C. The cost of capital with taxes

MEI: Section C asks what the lecture eight formula for the cost of equity becomes with taxes. Permanent debt, no growth, every year the same. Three slides of algebra, 30 to 32, and here's the shape.

MIGUEL: Shape me.

MEI: Step one: what shareholders earn each year is the operating income minus the interest, after tax, divided by the value of their shares. Step two: the after-tax operating income is what the unlevered firm pays its owners, so it equals the unlevered cost of capital times the unlevered value; and by MM2 Proposition 1, the unlevered value is the levered value minus the tax shield, that is, equity plus debt minus the tax rate times the debt. Step three: substitute step two into step one, open the bracket, divide through by the equity, and factor. What drops out is the cost of equity with taxes: the unlevered cost plus D over E times one minus the tax rate times the gap between the unlevered cost and the cost of debt.

MIGUEL: Same shape as lecture eight.

MEI: The slide puts them side by side. Same shape, one extra factor, one minus the tax rate, smaller than one. Intuition: interest is partly paid by the government, so debt takes less from shareholders in bad years. Equity still gets riskier with leverage, but more slowly. Example: unlevered cost ten percent, debt five, D over E one, tax twenty-five percent. With taxes: ten plus one times zero point seven five times five, thirteen point seven five percent. Without taxes: fifteen.

MIGUEL: The effective cost of debt.

MEI: Because debt saves tax, its effective cost is lower than the interest. Debt of a hundred thousand euros at ten percent, tax thirty-four percent. Interest ten thousand. Tax saving: thirty-four percent of ten thousand, three thousand four hundred. Effective cost: six thousand six hundred. As a rate, six point six percent, which is ten percent times one minus zero point three four. The after-tax cost of debt, the rate that enters the WACC in lecture six.

MIGUEL: And the WACC with taxes.

MEI: The WACC of a levered firm is debt weight times the after-tax cost of debt plus equity weight times the cost of equity. The slide splits it: the first two terms are the pre-tax WACC, the last term is what the tax shield saves. And if you plug in the cost of equity with taxes, slide 38 gives the result in a box: the levered WACC equals the unlevered WACC times one minus the tax rate times the debt share. Without taxes the WACC stayed flat. With taxes it falls as the share of debt rises.

MIGUEL: The picture.

MEI: Cost of equity rising with D over E, more slowly than in lecture eight. After-tax cost of debt, the cheapest source, flat at the bottom. And the WACC sloping down between them as more weight goes on the cheap, tax-deductible debt.

MIGUEL: Betas with taxes?

MEI: One slide. Levered beta equals unlevered beta times one plus one minus the tax rate times D over E. Lecture eight had one plus D over E; taxes add the same factor as in the cost of equity. Example: unlevered beta one, D over E one, tax twenty-five percent: one times one plus zero point seven five, one point seven five, against two without taxes. And unlevering goes the same way backwards, dividing instead of multiplying.

## Beyond MM2: uncertain debt and the two WACCs

MEI: MM2 assumed perpetual, constant debt, so the shield could be discounted at the risk-free rate. If the future level of leverage is unknown, the tax savings are risky, they need a higher discount rate, and their present value is lower than the tax rate times D. The general statement stays: V levered equals V unlevered plus the present value of the shield, whatever that is.

MIGUEL: And the shortcut for the special case.

MEI: A target debt-equity ratio: the firm keeps D over E constant, so its debt grows with the firm. Then value the firm twice. Once at the pre-tax WACC, debt at its full cost: that's V unlevered. Once at the after-tax WACC, debt at its after-tax cost: that's V levered. The gap is the value of the tax shield. With a free cash flow growing at a rate g, each value is the cash flow over the WACC minus g.

MIGUEL: Western Lumber.

MEI: Example 15.3. Free cash flow four point two five million next year, growing four percent a year, D over E of zero point five, so equity is one over one point five of the firm and debt zero point five over one point five. Cost of equity ten percent, debt six, tax twenty-one. Step one, pre-tax WACC: one over one point five times ten plus zero point five over one point five times six, eight point six seven percent. V unlevered: four point two five over eight point six seven minus four, ninety-one million. Step two, after-tax WACC: same with the debt cost times zero point seven nine, eight point two five percent. V levered: four point two five over eight point two five minus four, a hundred million. Step three: the shield is worth nine million.

MIGUEL: Nine million from a difference of zero point four two in the discount rate. That's a lot of leverage in a small number.

MEI: That's the thing about growing perpetuities: the value is the cash flow over a small gap between the rate and the growth, so a little change in the rate moves the value a lot. And compare with the permanent-debt formula: here the debt is a third of a hundred million, about thirty-three, and twenty-one percent of that would be seven. We got nine, not seven, because with a target ratio the debt grows with the firm, so the shield grows too. Different assumption, different number, same logic.

## The leveraged recapitalization: Micron SA

MEI: Now the example that makes the theory visible on a share price. A leveraged recap: the firm borrows and uses the money to buy back its own shares. Assets don't change, only the financing. Micron SA has no debt, twenty million shares at fifteen euros, tax rate thirty-three percent. The CEO decides to borrow a hundred million permanently and buy back shares. What happens to firm value, and to the price?

MIGUEL: Initial.

MEI: Twenty million shares times fifteen, three hundred million. Step one, the announcement. The future tax shield is worth thirty-three percent of a hundred, thirty-three million, and the market prices it at once: equity three hundred and thirty-three, price three thirty-three over twenty, sixteen euros sixty-five. Step two, the debt is issued: the firm holds a hundred of cash and owes a hundred, equity unchanged at three thirty-three. Step three, the buyback at sixteen sixty-five: a hundred over sixteen point six five, six point zero zero six million shares bought back, leaving thirteen point nine nine four million. Equity: thirteen point nine nine four times sixteen point six five, two hundred and thirty-three.

MIGUEL: So the price jumps at the announcement, not at the buyback.

MEI: That's the box, and it's the exam question. The price goes from fifteen to sixteen sixty-five when the recap is announced. Shareholders capture the whole tax shield: twenty million shares times one euro sixty-five, thirty-three million. The price can't stay at fifteen, because buying shares at fifteen that will be worth sixteen sixty-five would be free money. The ones who sell in the buyback and the ones who stay get the same sixteen sixty-five.

MIGUEL: Let me try the wrong answer, so people recognize it. The firm buys back at fifteen, gets more shares for its hundred million, and the remaining shareholders enjoy the whole shield.

MEI: That's the tempting version, and it fails for the same reason as every fallacy in this course: the Law of One Price. If the buyback happened at fifteen, anyone could buy at fifteen the moment the recap is announced and sell at sixteen sixty-five afterwards. Everyone would, so the price moves at the announcement. The sellers in the buyback aren't the losers and the stayers aren't the winners; the news is the event, and the news is public.

MIGUEL: Same lesson as lecture five, then. Public news is in the price the same day.

MEI: Same lesson. The course keeps rhyming.

## D. Personal taxes

MEI: Section D opens with a reference: Merton Miller, "Debt and Taxes," Journal of Finance, 1977. His observation: between 1930 and 1950 the marginal corporate tax rate in the US went from ten to fifty-two percent, and the liabilities of US companies didn't change significantly. If the tax shield were as big as MM2 says, firms should have loaded up on debt. They didn't. Miller's explanation: MM2 overstates the saving, because investors pay taxes too, on dividends, capital gains and interest.

MIGUEL: So Miller spent 1958 proving debt doesn't matter, and 1977 explaining why it matters less than his own 1963 correction said.

MEI: Which is the mark of a good scientist: he kept checking his own theory against the data and refined it twice. The 1977 paper is a classic of the kind we usually cover on this podcast, so if you want one original paper from this lecture to read, that's the one.

MIGUEL: Follow one euro.

MEI: One euro of EBIT. Route one, paid as interest: no corporate tax, the lender pays personal tax on interest at a rate tau i and keeps one minus tau i. Route two, paid to shareholders: the firm pays corporate tax first and keeps one minus tau C, then the shareholder pays tau e on dividends or capital gains and keeps one minus tau C times one minus tau e. Debt still wins if one minus tau i is the larger of the two.

MIGUEL: Numbers.

MEI: Corporate tax twenty-five percent, equity tax thirty, interest tax forty. Interest route: one, then zero point six zero after personal tax. Equity route: zero point seven five after corporate tax, then zero point seven five times zero point seven, zero point five two five. Debt still wins, but by zero point six minus zero point five two five, zero point zero seven five per euro, not by the full twenty-five cents of corporate tax saved.

MIGUEL: The effective tax advantage.

MEI: One formula, and we give it in words. The effective tax advantage of debt, tau star, is one minus the equity route divided by the interest route. In the example: one minus zero point five two five over zero point six, twelve point five percent. Without personal taxes it collapses to the corporate rate, twenty-five. Personal taxes fall more heavily on interest than on equity income, so part of the corporate saving is handed back to the tax authority at the investor's level. With permanent debt, leverage adds tau star times D instead of tau C times D. That tau star is the slope in the trade-off figure.

## E. Optimal capital structure with taxes

MEI: Section E. Two questions that turn out to be one: what leverage maximizes firm value, and what leverage minimizes the WACC? Firm value is the free cash flows, which don't depend on leverage, discounted at the WACC. So the leverage that minimizes the WACC also maximizes value.

MIGUEL: And the low leverage puzzle.

MEI: What MM2 predicts: every euro of debt adds tau star of value and lowers the WACC, so a firm should borrow until interest uses up all its taxable income. What we observe: large US and French firms finance only about twenty percent of their assets with debt. Interest absorbs well under half of EBIT in most years, and few firms report losses. Most could deduct much more interest than they do. The puzzle: firms leave tax savings on the table. Either the benefit is smaller than it looks, personal taxes, interest that can't be deducted, or debt has costs that MM2 ignores: financial distress, agency problems.

MIGUEL: The evidence.

MEI: Three charts. Net external financing of US corporations, 1980 to 2021: when firms raise money outside, it's mostly debt; net equity is usually negative, firms buy back more than they issue; and most investment is paid from internal cash. Debt-to-value of US firms, 1975 to 2021: between about twenty and fifty percent, and falling since 2010, large companies only. And interest as a percentage of EBIT for the S&P 500, 1975 to 2021, next to the share of firms with negative pre-tax income: interest well under half of EBIT in most years, few firms with losses. Firms could deduct much more.

MIGUEL: The limit example.

MEI: Interest saves tax only on what can be deducted: up to profit, and in the US up to thirty percent of EBIT. EBIT a thousand, tax twenty-one percent, limit three hundred. No debt: taxes two hundred and ten. Moderate debt, interest three hundred, all deducted: taxable income seven hundred, taxes a hundred and forty-seven, saving sixty-three. Excess debt, interest five hundred, only three hundred deducted: still seven hundred taxable, still a hundred and forty-seven of tax, still sixty-three saved. The extra two hundred of interest saves nothing. The tax-optimal interest is the limit itself.

MIGUEL: And the figure with the two lines.

MEI: With the limit at EBIT itself, a thousand: up to it, each euro of interest saves tau star, the red line rises. Beyond it, extra interest saves no corporate tax but is still taxed in the lender's hands, so the slope turns negative. And with risky EBIT, the blue line, the firm may not earn enough to use all its deductions, so the best interest level is lower, about nine hundred. Borrow up to the point where you're sure to use the deduction, not beyond.

MIGUEL: How to explain the low leverage, then.

MEI: Two factors MM left out. Bankruptcy costs: the possibility of bankruptcy on its own doesn't break MM, but if bankruptcy has costs, direct or indirect, the cash flows left for investors shrink. Agency costs: the capital structure changes how the company is managed, so it changes the free cash flows, contradicting MM's assumption; the effect can be positive or negative. The trade-off figure: at low debt, V levered rises with slope tau star, the tax benefit; as debt grows, expected financial distress costs grow faster and pull value down. The best debt level is where one more euro of debt adds as much in distress costs as in tax savings. Higher distress costs mean less debt.

MIGUEL: And the other theory.

MEI: Two named theories. Trade-off theory: the optimal structure weighs the tax benefit of debt against the costs of financial distress. Pecking order theory, linked to signalling: managers prefer retained earnings first, then debt, and issue equity only as a last resort. Both come back in lecture ten.

## Wrap-up: the recap

MEI: Lecture nine in one minute. Interest is deductible, dividends are not. Borrow eighty at five percent with thirty-four percent tax: net income drops from six point six to three point nine six, but investors as a group get seven point nine six instead of six point six. The gap, one point three six, is the interest tax shield, tax rate times interest. With permanent debt the shield is worth tax rate times debt: twenty-seven point two on eighty, seventy-five on two fifty, so a firm worth five hundred becomes five seventy-five.

MIGUEL: The cost of capital.

MEI: The cost of equity still rises with D over E, but with a factor one minus the tax rate: thirteen point seven five instead of fifteen in the example. The after-tax cost of debt is the rate times one minus the tax rate, six point six instead of ten. The WACC falls with leverage. Betas get the same factor: one point seven five instead of two. With a target debt-equity ratio, value the firm at the pre-tax and at the after-tax WACC and take the difference: nine million for Western Lumber. Micron's recap: the shield of thirty-three is priced on announcement, fifteen to sixteen sixty-five.

MIGUEL: Personal taxes and the puzzle.

MEI: Investors pay tax too, and more on interest than on equity income, so the advantage shrinks to tau star: twelve and a half percent in the example instead of twenty-five. Firms borrow far less than MM2 suggests, about twenty percent of assets, because interest beyond the limit saves nothing, because personal taxes eat part of the saving, and because debt brings financial distress and agency costs. The best debt level balances the tax saving against those costs.

MIGUEL: The quiz.

MEI: Why does debt raise firm value with corporate taxes but not in a perfect market? Interest is deducted before corporate tax, so the government takes less of EBIT and investors receive more; in a perfect market there's no tax to save. A firm worth a thousand with twenty-five percent tax borrows two hundred permanently and buys back shares: a thousand plus a quarter of two hundred, a thousand and fifty, and the shareholders capture the fifty when the recap is announced. Why don't firms borrow as much as MM2 suggests? Interest above taxable income or above the legal limit saves no tax, personal taxes reduce the advantage to tau star, and too much debt brings financial distress costs.
