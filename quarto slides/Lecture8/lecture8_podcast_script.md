## A. MM Proposition II

MEI: The proposition in the box: the cost of levered equity is the unlevered cost of capital, plus a premium that grows with the debt-to-equity ratio. In symbols, levered cost of equity equals unlevered cost plus D over E times the difference between the unlevered cost and the cost of debt. The second term is the price of leverage. Zero without debt, growing with every euro of debt per euro of equity.

MIGUEL: And the three steps to get there.

MEI: Here's the shape, the algebra is on slides 18 to 21. Step one: write MM I as an equation. Levered equity plus debt equals the unlevered value equals the assets. Step two: a portfolio holding all the debt and all the equity receives every cash flow of the assets, so its return is the unlevered return. And a portfolio's return is the weighted average of what's in it: equity weight times the equity return plus debt weight times the debt return equals the unlevered return.

MIGUEL: Step three.

MEI: Solve that for the equity return. The professor does it in four moves, a to d: multiply through so the equity return stands alone, split a fraction, move the debt term across, factor out D over E. What comes out is the return on levered equity equals the unlevered return plus D over E times the gap between the unlevered return and the debt return. He calls the second term "an extra kick," which is the right name. And since the identity holds for realized returns, it holds for expected returns too, which is MM II.

MIGUEL: So the four moves are just rearranging one weighted average.

MEI: Just rearranging one weighted average. If you can do step three with a pencil once, you'll never need to memorize MM II again, because you can rebuild it from "debt plus equity is the business."

MIGUEL: And Tour moche?

MEI: Two slides with real numbers. First, step two checked. Debt five hundred, pays five twenty-five in both states, return plus five percent. ## C. Levered and unlevered betas

MEI: Section C says: everything we just did with returns, do it with betas. Beta is a measure of risk exposure. Changing the capital structure moves risk between lenders and shareholders; it doesn't create or destroy it. So the firm's asset beta doesn't change with the capital structure. What changes is the equity beta.

MIGUEL: The two formulas.

MEI: Same shapes as section A. The unlevered beta is the weighted average of the levered equity beta and the debt beta, weights E over E plus D and D over E plus D. And turned around, the levered equity beta equals the unlevered beta plus D over E times the gap between the unlevered beta and the debt beta. Unlevered beta: the market risk of the business as if it had no debt. Levered beta: business risk plus the risk added by leverage.

MIGUEL: And the derivation?

MEI: Two slides, 30 and 31, same three steps as for returns. Hold all the debt and all the equity: same risk as the unlevered firm. A portfolio's beta is the weighted average of the betas, lecture four. Solve for the equity beta: multiply through, move the debt term, split the fraction. The one sentence to keep is on slide 31: the equity carries the business risk, minus the part the lenders take, spread over the euros of equity alone.

MIGUEL: And if the debt is risk free?

MEI: Then the debt beta is zero and the second formula collapses to the one you'll use ninety percent of the time: levered beta equals unlevered beta times one plus D over E. Intuition: each euro of equity controls E plus D over E euros of assets, and carries that many times the business risk.

MIGUEL: The first example.

MEI: A firm with a debt-to-equity ratio of two, equity beta one point seven, risk-free debt. What's the asset beta? One point seven equals the unlevered beta times one plus two, so the unlevered beta is one point seven over three, zero point five seven. Each euro of equity controls three euros of assets, so the stock is three times as risky as the business.

MIGUEL: And back the other way.

MEI: A firm in the same industry has a debt ratio, debt over debt plus equity, of zero point five. Same business, same asset beta zero point five seven. First convert: debt is half the firm, so debt equals equity, D over E is one. Then relever: zero point five seven times one plus one, one point one three. Less leverage than the first firm, so less risky stock, one point one three instead of one point seven, but still riskier than the business at zero point five seven. Unlever, then relever: that two-way move is the whole of section C.

## D. Two fallacies

MEI: Section D. Within MM, capital structure doesn't affect value. And yet two wrong arguments for leverage keep coming back. One: leverage and earnings per share. Leverage can raise expected EPS, so some shareholders conclude it should raise the stock price. But the risk of earnings changes too, and the higher average EPS is exactly what compensates shareholders for it. In perfect markets, the price doesn't change.

MIGUEL: Two: dilution.

MEI: Dilution is an increase in the number of shares dividing a fixed amount of earnings. Issuing equity usually dilutes EPS, and people argue that it dilutes existing shareholders, so the firm should borrow instead. In fact there's no dilution of value if the shares are sold at market value. Any gain or loss comes from the NPV of what the firm does with the money. And the price drop often seen when a capital increase is announced is an information effect, a friction, not dilution.

MIGUEL: And the summary of MM.

MEI: Perfect capital markets imply conservation of value. Financial transactions neither add nor destroy value; they repackage risk, and therefore return. The cost of capital depends on the assets of the firm and their risk. MM for dummies, in the professor's words: the total amount of pizza doesn't change if you slice it differently. Practical implications: investment decisions matter more than financing decisions, and it's easier to create or destroy value with investments than with financing.

## Exercise 3: Perkscom and Mr. Goodman

MEI: Exercise three is a short story, and Professor Saldarriaga gave it ten slides, so you know he enjoyed it. Mr. Goodman, CFO of Perkscom, comes back thoughtful from the shareholder meeting. The investment fund that owns ten percent of the firm wants an earnings yield of at least twenty percent this year, or it takes away the managers' stock options. MIGUEL: So the stakes are a villa.

MEI: The stakes are a villa. The facts: Perkscom's cash flow equals its EBIT, ten million this year, growing ten percent a year for ever. No leverage. Book equity eighty million, market cap one hundred million. It can borrow at five percent. No taxes, and it pays out all its cash flow as dividends.

MIGUEL: And the earnings yield is?

MEI: Earnings over the market value of equity. Today: ten over one hundred, ten percent. Compare the accounting return on equity, which divides by book value: ten over eighty, twelve point five. Same numerator, different denominator. The fund wants twenty, twice the current yield.

MIGUEL: Question one, the cost of capital.

MEI: The firm pays out everything, so it's worth the present value of its dividends: ten million next year, growing ten percent for ever. And note: the earnings yield of ten percent is the cost of capital minus the growth rate, twenty minus ten. A low yield here is the price of growth, not a sign of poor performance.

MIGUEL: Question two, the reorganization.

MEI: Cash flow fifteen million, same growth, same risk, same twenty percent. Value: fifteen over twenty minus ten, one hundred and fifty million. Shareholders gain fifty million of real value. MIGUEL: Question six, the lowest leverage that gets to twenty.
