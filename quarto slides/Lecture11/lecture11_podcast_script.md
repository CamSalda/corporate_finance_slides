## Recap of lecture ten: payout policy

MEI: Lecture ten was about what a firm does with spare cash. There are two ways to give it to shareholders. A dividend pays cash on every share. A repurchase means the firm buys back some of its own shares.

MIGUEL: And the effect on the share price is different, right?

MEI: It is. With a dividend, the share price falls by the amount of the dividend. With a repurchase, the price stays the same but the number of shares falls. Either way, a shareholder ends with the same wealth: a share plus cash in one case, or more valuable shares, or cash from selling, in the other.

MIGUEL: Remind me of the four dates of a dividend. I always mix them up.

MEI: Declaration: the board announces the dividend. Ex-dividend: from this day on, buyers of the share do not get it, so the price falls by the dividend on that day. Record date: the register fixes who is paid. Payable date: the cash goes out.

MIGUEL: And in a perfect market the price falls by exactly the dividend.

MEI: Exactly the dividend. Two ratios to remember as well. The dividend yield is the annual dividend divided by the stock price: the cash return you get each year from dividends, per euro invested in the share. The payout ratio is cash dividends divided by earnings per share: the share of earnings paid out, the rest being retained.

MIGUEL: And then the big theorem.

MEI: Modigliani and Miller, 1961. With investments fixed, in a perfect market, payout policy does not change the share price. The reason is homemade dividends: investors can create any payout pattern they like by buying or selling shares themselves, so they do not pay more for one policy than for another.

MIGUEL: But in practice it does matter.

MEI: Three reasons from the lecture. Taxes: where dividends are taxed more than capital gains, repurchases are cheaper for shareholders. Clientele effects: the tax cost of a dividend differs across investors, so each payout policy attracts the investors it suits. And the pay-out-or-retain question: pay out the cash the firm cannot invest in positive-NPV projects, because retained cash costs tax and invites waste, although it saves the cost of raising money later.

MIGUEL: And signalling.

MEI: Firms smooth dividends, so a change is news. Paying high dividends is costly if the finances are not solid, so an increase signals that managers are confident about future cash flows, and a cut signals the opposite. Repurchases are a positive signal too, because managers can raise the firm's value by buying back undervalued shares. That's the recap. Now lecture eleven.

## A. Equity financing for private companies

MEI: Section A. The initial capital to start a business usually comes from the entrepreneur and the immediate family. But to grow, a private company often needs outside money, and the key thing to understand is how outside capital affects control of the company.

MIGUEL: Who are the outsiders?

MEI: Five types. First, angel investors: individuals who buy equity in small private firms. The slides note that finding angels is typically difficult.

MIGUEL: They don't advertise.

MEI: Second, venture capital firms. This one has a structure worth learning. A VC firm raises a fund from investors called the limited partners: pension funds, endowments, wealthy families. Its managers, the general partners, invest that fund in start-ups.

MIGUEL: Why would a pension fund go through a VC rather than invest in start-ups directly?

MEI: Two advantages: the limited partners are more diversified, and they benefit from the expertise of the general partners. The catch is the fees. Most firms charge twenty percent of any positive return they make, that's called carried interest, plus an annual management fee of about two percent of the fund's committed capital.

MIGUEL: Two and twenty. So on a hundred-million fund, two million a year just for showing up.

MEI: Just for managing it, yes. Third type: private equity firms. Organized very much like a venture capital firm, but they invest in the equity of existing privately held firms rather than start-ups. They may also find a publicly traded firm, buy all the outstanding equity, and take it private. That's a leveraged buyout, an LBO.

MIGUEL: Leveraged meaning with debt.

MEI: Mostly debt. The slide puts it this way: the private equity firm buys all the shares and pays for most of the price with borrowed money, often sixty percent or more. The debt is then carried by the company itself and repaid out of its own cash flows. The private equity firm puts in only a small slice of equity, so if the company does well, its return is large. That's the leverage effect from lecture eight. If the company struggles, the heavy debt can push it into financial distress.

MIGUEL: So the company borrows to buy itself, on behalf of its new owner.

MEI: That's a fair way to put it. Fourth type: institutional investors, like pension funds, insurance companies, endowments and foundations. They may invest directly in private firms or indirectly, as limited partners in VC funds. And fifth: corporate investors, corporations that invest in private companies. Most investors care only about financial returns. Corporate investors may also have strategic objectives.

MIGUEL: A big tech firm investing in a start-up it might want to buy later.

MEI: For instance.

## Funding rounds: pre-money and post-money

MEI: Now the arithmetic of section A, which you will be tested on. A private firm raises money in rounds: Series A, B, C and so on. At each round, the investors and the firm agree on a value for the firm. Two values, in fact.

MIGUEL: Pre-money and post-money.

MEI: The pre-money value is what the firm is worth before the new money comes in. The post-money value is what it's worth after, cash included. And the relation is simple: post-money equals pre-money plus the amount invested.

MIGUEL: Because the firm now has the cash on top of what it had.

MEI: Exactly. And the new investors get a share of the firm equal to what they paid, divided by the post-money value. That's the control question: every round gives away part of the ownership.

MIGUEL: Walk me through the example.

MEI: Example 23.1 from the textbook. You start the firm and put in a hundred thousand dollars for one and a half million shares. Angels buy half a million shares, also for a hundred thousand dollars, by assumption. Then a VC invests six million dollars at a post-money value of ten million.

MIGUEL: So the VC's share is six over ten. Sixty percent.

MEI: Sixty percent. And the pre-money value was ten minus six: four million dollars. Before the VC, there were two million shares, yours and the angels', so each share is worth two dollars. Your one and a half million shares are now worth three million dollars.

MIGUEL: Three million, from a hundred thousand. Thirty times more.

MEI: But you control only thirty percent of the firm. That's the trade-off in one line: your hundred thousand dollars is now worth three million, but your majority is gone.

MIGUEL: And the real-world example on the slides?

MEI: AMI, Yann LeCun's start-up. In March 2026 it raised one point zero three billion dollars at a pre-money value of three point five billion. So the post-money value is three point five plus one point zero three: four point five three billion. The new investors' share is one point zero three over four point five three, about twenty-two point seven percent. Founders and earlier investors keep the rest, seventy-seven point three percent, of a firm now worth four point five three billion.

MIGUEL: So raising money dilutes you, but your slice can still be worth more.

MEI: That's the box on the slide: a smaller slice of a much bigger cake. One more detail before we leave private firms. VCs usually buy convertible preferred shares, which carry a liquidation preference. If the firm is sold cheaply, they get their money back before the founders. If it is sold at a high price, they convert into ordinary shares and take their percentage.

MIGUEL: Heads they win, tails they get their money back first.

MEI: Preferences protect investors when the firm does badly. That's the point.

## B. The initial public offering

MEI: Section B. An IPO is the process of selling stock to the public for the first time. Two advantages. Greater liquidity: the private equity investors can finally diversify. And better access to capital: public companies can raise much larger amounts through the public markets.

MIGUEL: And the disadvantages?

MEI: The equity holders become more widely dispersed, which makes it harder to monitor management. And the firm must satisfy all the requirements of public companies: SEC filings, Sarbanes-Oxley, and so on.

MIGUEL: Okay. What kinds of offerings are there?

MEI: Two distinctions. First, primary versus secondary. In a primary offering, new shares are sold and the money goes to the firm. In a secondary offering, existing shareholders sell their shares, so the money goes to them, not to the firm.

MIGUEL: And the second distinction?

MEI: How the bank, the underwriter, sells the shares. Best efforts: for smaller IPOs, the underwriter does not guarantee the stock will be sold, it just tries to get the best price. These deals often have an all-or-none clause: either all the shares are sold or the deal is called off. Firm commitment: the underwriter guarantees it will sell all the stock at the offer price. And an auction IPO: investors place bids, and the price is the highest price at which the number of bids at or above it equals the number of shares offered.

MIGUEL: So who bears the risk of unsold shares?

MEI: That's the key point. Best efforts: the firm. Firm commitment: the bank.

MIGUEL: And the examples on the slides are from this year.

MEI: Two from June 2026. Anthropic files to go public: an IPO, the first sale of its shares to the public. It's a firm commitment offer, with about twenty-two banks led by Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley buying all the shares and reselling them at a price set by book building. And Alphabet, already public, sells eighty billion dollars of new shares to pay for AI data centres. Existing shareholders are diluted.

MIGUEL: Same tool, new shares, at two stages of a firm's life.

MEI: That's the slide's sentence, word for word. Then there's a table of the largest US IPOs. The top is SpaceX in June 2026, on Nasdaq, led by Goldman Sachs: seventy-five billion dollars. Then SK hynix in July 2026, about twenty-six and a half billion. Alibaba in 2014, nearly twenty-two billion. Visa in 2008, Facebook in 2012, General Motors in 2010, each around sixteen billion.

MIGUEL: Those are monsters.

MEI: And the slide reminds you that a typical IPO is far smaller: the median US IPO raised about a hundred and eighty million dollars in 2020.

## The IPO, step by step

MIGUEL: Okay, how does an IPO actually happen?

MEI: Six steps. One: choose the banks, a lead underwriter and a syndicate. The lead underwriter is the primary investment bank responsible for managing the issuance. The syndicate is a group of underwriters who jointly underwrite and distribute it.

MIGUEL: Step two?

MEI: File with the regulator, the SEC in the United States. The registration statement is the legal document with financial and other information about the company. Part of it, the preliminary prospectus, is circulated to investors before the stock is offered. It's nicknamed the red herring.

MIGUEL: Why red herring?

MEI: Because of the red warning printed on its cover saying it's not final. Which brings us to the final prospectus: the version with all the details of the offering, including the number of shares and the offer price. Step three: value the firm and set a price range. Two methods: compute the present value of the estimated future cash flows, or look at comparables, like recent IPOs.

MIGUEL: Step four is the fun one. The road show.

MEI: Senior management and the underwriters travel around promoting the company and explaining the rationale for the offer price to the underwriters' largest customers, mainly institutional investors like mutual funds and pension funds. Alongside the road show comes book building: the underwriters collect those investors' expressions of interest and use them to set the offer price.

MIGUEL: So the price comes from the order book, not from a formula.

MEI: From the book. Step five: set the offer price, publish the final prospectus, and the shares start trading. And here the underwriter's fee appears: the spread, a percentage of the issue price per share.

MIGUEL: And in a firm commitment, the bank could lose money if the shares sell below the offer price.

MEI: It could, in principle. But research shows that seventy-five percent of IPOs see their share price rise on the first day, and only nine percent see a decrease. So the bank's risk is usually small.

MIGUEL: Step six, after the IPO.

MEI: Two things. The greenshoe provision, also called the over-allotment allocation: an option that allows the underwriter to issue more stock, usually fifteen percent of the original offer size, at the IPO offer price, during the first thirty days.

MIGUEL: Why would the bank want that?

MEI: Here's the mechanism, and it's clever. The underwriter initially sells both the original allotment and the greenshoe allotment, so it is short the extra fifteen percent. If the issue is a success and the price rises, the underwriter exercises the greenshoe option, gets the extra shares from the firm at the offer price, and covers its short position. If the issue is not a success and the price falls, the underwriter buys the shares back in the market to cover the short, which supports the price.

MIGUEL: So the greenshoe is a price stabilizer either way.

MEI: Either way. The second thing after the IPO is the lockup: a restriction that prevents insiders from selling their shares for some period, usually ninety to a hundred and eighty days.

MIGUEL: So the founders can't cash out on day one.

MEI: Not for three to six months.

## The four IPO puzzles

MEI: Now chapter 23.3, the four IPO puzzles. Four facts that do not fit a perfect market.

MIGUEL: Puzzle one?

MEI: Underpricing. On average, the share closes its first day well above the offer price, by more than seventeen percent in the United States since 1960. The issuer leaves money on the table.

MIGUEL: Seventeen percent in a day. If I'm the firm, I just sold my shares too cheap.

MEI: That's the puzzle. Two: hot and cold markets. IPOs come in waves. Many firms go public when markets are high, like 1983, 1996, and 1999 to 2000, and almost none after a crash, like 2001 to 2003 and 2008. Three: high costs. An IPO costs about seven percent of the money raised in fees alone, far more than issuing bonds or seasoned equity. Four: long-run underperformance. In the three to five years after an IPO, the new shares tend to do worse than the market.

MIGUEL: And the explanations?

MEI: The slide gives the usual ones. For underpricing, the winner's curse: if you're an uninformed investor, you get all the shares you asked for in the bad IPOs, because the informed investors stayed away, and only a few in the good ones. To keep you bidding at all, IPOs must be underpriced on average. For the other three puzzles, managers timing the market: issuing when prices are high, which explains the waves, and which also means the shares then underperform.

MIGUEL: So the firm's insiders know more than the buyers. That theme again.

MEI: It will come back in a few minutes with seasoned offerings.

## C. SPACs and seasoned equity offerings

MEI: Section C. First, SPACs, which the lecture calls going public through the back door. A SPAC, a special purpose acquisition company, is an empty company that goes public first, with a promise: buy a private firm within two years. The private firm then becomes public by merging with the SPAC, with no IPO of its own.

MIGUEL: So the shell does the IPO, and the real company climbs in afterwards.

MEI: That's it. The example is the electric-car maker Lucid, which merged with the SPAC CCIV in 2021. Fees were three point seven percent of the money raised, about half the seven percent of an IPO. The first-day price was twenty-six dollars eighty-three, against an effective price of nine dollars eighty-seven a share, so the shares rose a hundred and seventy-two percent.

MIGUEL: Cheaper and a huge pop. What's the catch?

MEI: After the merger, SPAC firms have done badly: on average almost twenty-five percent a year below the market. And this was not a niche: more than a third of 2021 listings were SPACs.

MIGUEL: Okay. Seasoned equity offerings.

MEI: An SEO is how a public firm raises additional equity. The steps are similar to an IPO, with one big difference: a market price for the stock already exists, so the whole price-setting process is unnecessary.

MIGUEL: And there are two kinds.

MEI: A cash offer, where new shares are offered to investors at large. And a rights offer, where new shares are offered only to existing shareholders. The slide says the rights offer protects existing shareholders from underpricing and wealth transfer. Let me show you why with the lecture's example, because this one is worth working through step by step.

MIGUEL: The Alpha example.

MEI: Alpha has no debt and one asset worth a hundred euros. Its capital is fifty shares, so each share is worth two euros. Alpha announces it will issue fifty new shares at one euro each. The low price guarantees the issue succeeds.

MIGUEL: So after the issue, the firm has a hundred euros of old assets plus fifty euros of new cash. A hundred and fifty euros, and a hundred shares.

MEI: So each share is worth one euro fifty. Now follow the money. The old shareholders had fifty shares worth two euros each: a hundred euros. After, they have fifty shares worth one fifty: seventy-five euros. They lost twenty-five euros.

MIGUEL: And the new shareholders paid fifty euros for fifty shares now worth seventy-five. They gained twenty-five.

MEI: Exactly the twenty-five the old shareholders lost. Selling shares below their value moves wealth from old shareholders to new ones. That's the wealth transfer.

MIGUEL: And the rights offer fixes it.

MEI: Suppose instead each existing shareholder receives the right to buy one additional share for one euro. If they all exercise, the firm is again worth a hundred and fifty euros with a hundred shares, so the price is again one euro fifty. The share price fell by fifty cents, as before. But now the new shareholders are the old shareholders, so the loss on the old shares is compensated by the gain on the new ones.

MIGUEL: In numbers?

MEI: Together, the old shareholders had a hundred euros of shares and fifty euros of cash: a hundred and fifty. After, they have a hundred shares at one fifty: a hundred and fifty. No loss. The twenty-five euros stays with the same people.

MIGUEL: So with a rights offer the issue price doesn't matter.

MEI: And the lecture proves it with a second example, 23.8 in the textbook. A firm worth a billion dollars has a hundred million shares at ten dollars each. It needs two hundred million. Offer A: four rights plus eight dollars buy one new share. That's twenty-five million new shares, which raise twenty-five million times eight: two hundred million. The firm is then worth one point two billion with a hundred and twenty-five million shares, so nine dollars sixty a share.

MIGUEL: And offer B?

MEI: Five rights plus ten dollars buy two new shares. Forty million new shares at five dollars each: again two hundred million. One point two billion over a hundred and forty million shares: eight dollars fifty-seven a share.

MIGUEL: Different prices after. But the shareholder is equally well off?

MEI: Take a shareholder with four shares and eight dollars under offer A. Before: four times ten plus eight, forty-eight dollars. After: five shares at nine sixty, forty-eight dollars. The same holds under offer B. With a rights offer, the issue price only changes how the value is split between old and new shares, not who owns it.

MIGUEL: Nice. And how does the market react to an SEO?

MEI: Badly, on average. Researchers find the price falls by three to five percent at the announcement. The explanation is signalling and adverse selection, from chapter sixteen. A company that wants to protect its existing shareholders will sell new shares only at a price that correctly values or overvalues the firm. So investors infer from the decision to sell that the company is likely overvalued, and the price drops.

MIGUEL: The lemons logic. If you're selling, I wonder why.

MEI: But the slide adds that adverse selection cannot be the whole story. A rights issue mitigates the adverse selection problem, yet the price falls even then. And, as with IPOs, the stock tends to underperform the market after an SEO, which suggests the initial drop is not even big enough.

MIGUEL: So the market reacts, but not enough. Okay, that's part one. Debt?

## D. Corporate debt

MEI: Part two. A debt security is described by five characteristics: its face value, its issue price, its coupon rate, which can be fixed, variable or indexed, the coupon payment dates, and the maturity.

MIGUEL: Give me a real one.

MEI: Hertz, the car-rental firm, in 2005. Total face value one point eight billion dollars, in bonds of a thousand dollars each. Issued at par, meaning the issue price was a hundred percent of face value. Coupon rate eight point eight seven five percent a year, fixed. Coupons every six months. Maturity 2014.

MIGUEL: So each bond pays what?

MEI: Eight point eight seven five percent of a thousand, divided by two: forty-four dollars thirty-eight every six months, and the thousand dollars back in 2014.

MIGUEL: And if the bond is issued below face value?

MEI: Then it's an original issue discount bond, an OID. Part of the return comes from the price rising toward face value as maturity approaches.

MIGUEL: How does a firm issue debt?

MEI: Two markets. Private debt is not publicly traded. It's a larger market in aggregate than the public one. It avoids the cost of registration but is illiquid. Two segments: term loans, which are fixed-term bank loans, and private placements, bond issues sold to a small group of investors and not traded on a public market.

MIGUEL: And public debt?

MEI: Requires publishing a prospectus with the details of the offering, including the indenture, the formal debt agreement between issuer and bondholder. The slide's examples: Mistral borrows privately, Oracle taps the public market. A young firm borrows privately; a large, well-known firm can raise much bigger amounts in public.

MIGUEL: Now, seniority. This is the "who gets paid first" question.

MEI: Most corporate bonds are unsecured: notes, with maturity under ten years, and debentures. So the bondholder's priority in claiming assets in a default, the bond's seniority, matters a lot. The order is this. First, secured debt, backed by specific assets like a building or the car fleet: mortgage bonds and asset-backed bonds. Second, senior unsecured debt: notes and debentures paid before other debt. Third, subordinated, or junior, debt, paid only after the senior debt. Fourth, shareholders, with what is left.

MIGUEL: And junior debt pays more to compensate.

MEI: Hertz again: its senior notes paid eight point eight seven five percent, its subordinated notes ten and a half percent. The lower you stand in the line, the higher the interest rate.

## E. Other types of debt

MEI: Section E, other debt. First, sovereign debt, issued by national governments. An expanding market, thanks to public deficits. In France, public debt at the end of 2025 was a hundred and fifteen point six percent of GDP, three thousand four hundred and sixty point five billion euros.

MIGUEL: Up from?

MEI: From a hundred and twelve percent of GDP, three thousand two hundred and twenty-eight billion, a year earlier. An increase of two hundred and thirty-two point five billion euros in a single year.

MIGUEL: That's a lot of bonds.

MEI: The slides also show the menu of US Treasury securities, and one special kind: TIPS, Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities. These are standard fixed-rate coupon bonds, with maturities of five, ten and twenty years, with one difference: the outstanding principal is adjusted for inflation. So the coupon, a fixed rate applied to a growing principal, grows with prices too.

MIGUEL: And the last type, the famous one from 2008.

MEI: Asset-backed securities. Securitization transforms assets into financial securities that can be traded. The largest sector is mortgage-backed securities, backed by home mortgages. And a CDO, a collateralized debt obligation, is a re-securitization of other asset-backed securities, often divided into tranches with different repayment priority.

MIGUEL: Seniority again, but inside a security.

MEI: Same idea.

## F. Bond covenants

MEI: Section F. Covenants are restrictive clauses in a bond contract that stop the issuer from undercutting its ability to repay. For example, they may restrict management's ability to pay dividends, restrict further borrowing, or require a minimum amount of working capital.

MIGUEL: And if the issuer breaks one?

MEI: The bond goes into default. Lenders can ask for their money back or renegotiate.

MIGUEL: Why would a firm accept clauses like that?

MEI: Because covenants protect lenders against managers taking the cash out or piling on more debt. Lenders feel safer, so they ask for a lower interest rate. The firm trades flexibility for cheaper money.

## G. Repayment provisions and bonds with options

MEI: Section G. Issuers can repurchase outstanding bonds in the market before maturity. Or the bond can directly allow early repayment through a call provision: a callable bond. It gives the issuer the right, but not the obligation, to retire all outstanding bonds on or after a specific date, the call date, at the call price, which is generally set at or above face value and expressed as a percentage of it.

MIGUEL: When does a firm call?

MEI: When interest rates have fallen. It refinances at the lower rate and cuts its borrowing cost. If rates rise, there is no need to refinance, so it does nothing.

MIGUEL: So it's an option in the firm's favour.

MEI: Which is the general point of the last slides. Bonds are commonly sold with options that give the issuer or the investor extra rights. Options for the investor: puttable bonds, which let the holder retire or extend the bond, and convertible bonds, which can be exchanged for shares of the firm's stock. Option for the issuer: the callable bond.

MIGUEL: And the question on the slide: does a bond with an option have a lower or higher yield than an identical bond without it?

MEI: Think about who holds the option, because an option is worth something to whoever holds it. If the option is for the investor, puttable or convertible, the investor gets an extra right and so accepts a lower yield. If the option is for the issuer, callable, the firm gets the extra right and must pay a higher yield.

MIGUEL: Whoever gets the toy pays for it.

MEI: Convertibles deserve two more definitions. The conversion ratio is the number of shares received per bond, usually stated per thousand dollars of face value. The conversion price is the face value divided by the number of shares received. And a convertible bond carries an embedded warrant.

MIGUEL: What's a warrant?

MEI: A call option written by the company itself on new stock. When the holder exercises it, the company delivers the shares by issuing new ones. Convertible debt carries a lower interest rate precisely because it has that warrant inside.

MIGUEL: So a convertible is a bond plus a call option on the firm's shares, and the investor pays for the option with a lower coupon.

MEI: That's the whole idea in one sentence.

MIGUEL: Okay. Recap time. The whole lecture in one minute, section by section.

MIGUEL: B.

MEI: The IPO: underwriters, book building, greenshoe, lockup. Best efforts puts the risk on the firm, firm commitment on the bank. And the four puzzles: IPOs are underpriced on average, come in waves, cost about seven percent, and do poorly in the long run.

MIGUEL: C.

MIGUEL: D.

MIGUEL: E.

MIGUEL: F and G.
