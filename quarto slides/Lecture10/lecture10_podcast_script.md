## Introduction and A. Distributions to shareholders

MEI: A firm that generates more cash than it can invest well faces three questions. Pay out or keep? Dividend or repurchase? And what does the choice say, since investors read payout decisions as news? The benchmark is a perfect market; then taxes, issuance costs, agency problems and information decide.

MIGUEL: The tree.

MEI: Free cash flow is the cash the business generates after its operations and investments. Two branches. Retain it: invest in new projects, good as long as they have positive NPV, or add to the cash reserves. Pay it out: dividends, a cash payment per share to every shareholder, or share repurchases, where the firm buys its own shares from the shareholders who choose to sell.

MIGUEL: Types of dividends.

MEI: The professor splits them into true and fake. True dividends: the regular cash dividend, quarterly in the US, annually in France; the special cash dividend, a one-time payment usually much larger; and the optional stock dividend in France, scrip dividend in the UK, where you choose cash or shares. Fake dividends: the stock dividend, paid in shares, and the stock split, which divides each share into several. No cash leaves the firm in the fake ones.

MIGUEL: And the ratios.

MEI: Two. Dividend yield: annual dividend over the stock price, the cash return you get each year per euro invested. Payout ratio: cash dividends over earnings per share, the share of earnings paid out; the rest is retained.

## The four dates

MEI: Now the procedure, and the professor loves a procedure in numbered steps. Costco's special dividend of ten dollars a share in 2020. Step one, the declaration date, 16 November: the board announces the amount and the dates, and the firm is now legally bound to pay. Step two, the ex-dividend date, 1 December: the first day on which a buyer does not get the dividend. Step three, the record date, 2 December: whoever is in the register that day receives it. Step four, the payable date, 11 December: the cash is paid.

MIGUEL: Why is the ex-date one day before the record date?

MEI: Because a purchase takes time to be registered. If you buy on the ex-date, you're not in the register by the record date, so the seller keeps the dividend. For the share price, the ex-dividend date is the one that matters: that's when the dividend leaves the share.

MIGUEL: The French option.

MEI: The optional stock dividend, "only in France" says the slide title, with a timeline from Sanofi in 2011: general meeting on 6 May, ex-dividend date 16 May, then a conversion window until 3 June in which each shareholder chooses cash or new shares, and payment on 16 June. From a paper by David and Ginglinger, 2016, whose title gives away a later lesson: "When cutting dividends is not bad news."

MIGUEL: And Sodexo.

MEI: The French example for the four dates. Sodexo's dividend for 2016, one euro per share: declared by the general meeting on 24 January 2017, ex-dividend 6 February, record date 7 February, paid 8 February. In France the whole thing fits in a fortnight.

MIGUEL: GM's dividend history.

MEI: A figure from 1983 to 2008. Adjusted for the two-for-one split of 1989, GM paid seventy-five cents a share around 1990, cut to forty, then to twenty in the early nineties, climbed back to fifty by 1997 and held it for almost a decade, then cut to twenty-five in 2006. And two tall yellow bars: special dividends of three dollars twenty-seven in 1998 and thirteen dollars seventy-two in 1999. Keep that picture; it comes back when we talk about smoothing.

## Why the price falls by the dividend

MEI: In a perfect market, the price falls exactly by the dividend on the ex-date. Three steps. Step one, the day before: a buyer gets the dividend about to be paid and all the future ones, so the cum-dividend price is the dividend plus the present value of future dividends. Step two, the ex-dividend day: a buyer no longer gets today's dividend, so the price is just the present value of the future ones. Step three, the difference: the dividend.

MIGUEL: And why can't it be otherwise?

MEI: If the price fell by less, buy the day before, collect the dividend, sell the day after: free money. If by more, do the opposite. Neither trade can pay in a perfect market. And the shareholder loses nothing: a share worth the ex price plus cash equal to the dividend is still worth the cum price. The slide adds that with taxes on dividends, empirically the price falls by a bit less than the dividend. We'll see why in section C.

MIGUEL: Repurchases.

MEI: Same result: the company pays cash to investors, and cash and equity both shrink. The difference is where it shows. A dividend reduces the stock price but not the number of shares. A repurchase reduces the number of shares but not the price. And repurchases can have tax benefits, section C.

MIGUEL: How do they work in France?

MEI: The general meeting approves a plan stating the share of capital targeted, under ten percent, the length, under eighteen months, and the financing, cash or debt. Repurchased shares can be cancelled or held. Two procedures: a fixed-price tender, where the firm offers to buy a defined quantity at a premium during a defined period, and the open market repurchase at the current price on the exchange.

MIGUEL: And abroad?

MEI: Open market repurchases are the most common everywhere. Also the tender offer; the Dutch auction in the US, where the firm lists prices, shareholders say how many shares they'd sell at each, and the firm pays the lowest price that gets it the number it wants; and the targeted repurchase or block purchase from a major shareholder, prohibited in France. Careful with that last one: if a large premium is paid, as in greenmail, buying out a shareholder who threatens a takeover, the other shareholders suffer a wealth transfer.

## B. Dividends versus repurchases on a balance sheet

MEI: Section B starts with the simplest firm imaginable. Cash a hundred and fifty thousand, other assets eight hundred and fifty thousand, no debt, equity one million. A hundred thousand shares, so ten dollars a share. The firm wants to distribute a hundred thousand.

MIGUEL: As a dividend.

MEI: One dollar per share. Cash drops to fifty thousand, equity to nine hundred thousand, still a hundred thousand shares: nine dollars a share.

MIGUEL: As a repurchase.

MEI: A hundred thousand dollars at ten dollars buys ten thousand shares, which are cancelled. Cash fifty thousand, equity nine hundred thousand, ninety thousand shares: ten dollars a share. Same equity, nine hundred thousand either way. The repurchase keeps the price and cuts the number of shares; the dividend keeps the number of shares and cuts the price. Same total value, split differently between price and quantity.

MIGUEL: And a shareholder?

MEI: Take one who owns a thousand shares, worth ten thousand dollars. Dividend: she gets a thousand in cash and keeps a thousand shares at nine, nine thousand plus one thousand, ten thousand. Repurchase, she doesn't sell: a thousand shares at ten, ten thousand. Repurchase, but she wants cash: she sells a hundred shares at ten for a thousand and keeps nine hundred worth nine thousand, ten thousand again, with the same split as the dividend.

MIGUEL: So she can build either outcome herself.

MEI: That's the homemade dividend. Under the repurchase she sells a few shares to get cash; under the dividend she buys a thousand dollars of shares at nine and is back to all-shares. In a perfect market she's as rich either way, and can convert one payout into the other.

## Genron: one firm, three policies

MEI: The textbook's example, and the professor's third fictional firm after Tour moche and Perkscom. Genron: no debt, twenty million of excess cash today, constant free cash flows of forty-eight million a year for ever, cost of capital twelve percent, ten million shares. Step one, the business: forty-eight over zero point one two, four hundred million. Step two, the firm: four hundred plus twenty, four hundred and twenty million, forty-two dollars a share. Three proposals for the twenty million: pay it as a dividend now; pay no dividend and buy back shares; or pay an even bigger dividend financed by selling new shares.

MIGUEL: Policy one.

MEI: Twenty million over ten million shares: two dollars a share now, then forty-eight over ten, four dollars eighty every year after. Cum-dividend price: two plus four eighty over zero point one two, two plus forty, forty-two. Ex-dividend price: forty. On the balance sheet, cash goes from twenty to zero, other assets stay at four hundred, total from four twenty to four hundred, ten million shares, price from forty-two to forty. A holder has forty of share and two of cash: forty-two, as before.

MIGUEL: Policy two.

MEI: At forty-two a share, twenty million buys zero point four seven six million shares, leaving nine point five two four million. Cash to zero, total to four hundred, and four hundred over nine point five two four is forty-two. The price holds, because the assets lost twenty million and so did the number of shares, in the same proportion. The future dividends: forty-eight million over nine point five two four million shares, five dollars and four cents a share, and five point zero four over zero point one two is forty-two.

MIGUEL: So shareholders give up two dollars today.

MEI: And get twenty-four cents more every year for ever, and those are worth the same. The box flags the common mistake: "fewer shares, so the price must rise." No: the firm also has less cash. The two effects cancel exactly.

MIGUEL: Policy three.

MEI: The board wants to pay forty-eight million now, not twenty. The missing twenty-eight is raised by selling new shares at forty-two: zero point six six seven million new shares, ten point six six seven million in total. Dividend per share: forty-eight over ten point six six seven, four dollars fifty, now and every year after. Price: four fifty plus four fifty over zero point one two, four fifty plus thirty-seven fifty, forty-two. A bigger dividend today, smaller ones tomorrow, same forty-two. Raising money to pay it straight back out creates nothing.

MIGUEL: The table.

MEI: Table 17.1, three rows. Policy one: two dollars now, four eighty after. Policy two: nothing now, five oh four after. Policy three: four fifty now and after. Read across a row: a higher dividend today always comes with lower dividends later, because there are more shares to pay. Read down the price column: forty-two, forty-two, forty-two. The box: dividends determine the share price; the firm's choice of dividend policy does not. It only moves cash between today and tomorrow.

## The exercise: repurchase and DCF

MEI: Now the exercise, which is the exam in miniature. Firm X has a hundred thousand shares and a constant cash flow of ten dollars per share, cost of capital ten percent. Policy one: pay a dividend of ten dollars every year. Policy two: use the cash at the end of year one to repurchase shares, then pay a regular dividend. The firm thinks policy two will raise the dividend per share and therefore the share price. Show that this is wrong.

MIGUEL: Policy one.

MEI: Ten dollars a year for ever at ten percent: ten over zero point one, a hundred dollars a share. Check with the whole firm: a hundred thousand shares pay a million a year, a million over zero point one is ten million, a hundred a share.

MIGUEL: Policy two.

MEI: Four steps. Step one: the share price at the end of year one, just before the repurchase. A share is a claim on this year's ten dollars and on the future cash flows, which are worth a hundred as under policy one: a hundred and ten. Step two: the million of cash buys a million over a hundred and ten, nine thousand and ninety-one shares, leaving ninety thousand nine hundred and nine. Step three: from year two, the million a year is shared by ninety thousand nine hundred and nine shares, eleven dollars a share. Step four: eleven a year for ever is worth a hundred and ten at the end of year one, and a hundred and ten over one point one is a hundred today.

MIGUEL: So a hundred either way.

MEI: Policy one: ten, ten, ten, price a hundred. Policy two: zero, eleven, eleven, price a hundred. Policy two raises the dividend per share without raising the share price. That's exactly Modigliani and Miller's point, which the deck then states in general.

MIGUEL: The firm's reasoning fails where?

MEI: It counted the higher dividends from year two and forgot the dividend it skipped in year one. Eleven for ever starting a year later is worth exactly ten for ever starting now, at ten percent. Nothing is free.

## MM and dividend irrelevance

MEI: When a firm sets its dividend, it takes two decisions at once: what fraction of earnings to pay out, and what to reinvest. Modigliani and Miller separated the two, and in 1961 got the dividend irrelevance theorem. In the box: in perfect capital markets, holding fixed the investment policy of a firm, the firm's choice of dividend policy is irrelevant and does not affect the initial share price.

MIGUEL: Intuition.

MEI: A dividend lowers the value of the shares by exactly the amount paid out, so the shareholders' wealth doesn't move. And dividends must be paid with cash flows, which are set by the investment policy. A dividend policy that leaves investments unchanged only changes when shareholders receive their money, not the present value of it.

MIGUEL: The argument in steps.

MEI: Four. One: the cash flows are fixed by the investments, so payout only decides when and in what form the cash arrives. Two: a dividend moves cash, it doesn't create it; the price falls by the dividend. Three: buying or selling shares at the market price is a zero-NPV trade, the dilution fallacy of lecture eight again. Four: shareholders can undo any policy at home. Want more cash? Sell a few shares. Want less? Reinvest the dividend. And the "bird in the hand" objection, that a dividend now beats the same value later, fails at step four: the shareholder can take the bird herself by selling.

MIGUEL: Professor Saldarriaga seems to enjoy knocking down a fallacy per lecture.

MEI: He does, and it's good teaching: the fallacies are what you'll hear in meetings. The fifteen percent discount rate in lecture seven, EPS and dilution in lecture eight, "fewer shares so the price rises" and "bird in the hand" today.

## C. Payout policy in practice

MEI: Section C, the frictions. First an overview slide. Reasons for low dividends: income tax, and the high cost of raising capital. Reasons for high dividends: asymmetric information, since dividends can signal performance, and clientele effects, since some investors pay less tax on dividends. And what we observe: firms try to pay regular dividends, more stable than earnings, which is dividend smoothing, and they try to maintain a constant payout ratio. Then each point in turn.

MIGUEL: Taxes.

MEI: Shareholders pay tax on dividends at one rate and on capital gains at another. A dividend is taxed at once. A repurchase lets each shareholder choose: sell and pay capital gains tax, or hold and pay nothing yet. When dividends are taxed more than capital gains, repurchases are cheaper for shareholders, and the best policy is to pay no dividend at all. The US top federal rates: 1971 to 78, capital gains thirty-five percent, dividends seventy. 1997 to 2002, twenty against thirty-nine or forty. 2003 to 2012, fifteen and fifteen. Since 2013, twenty and twenty.

MIGUEL: Seventy percent on dividends in the seventies.

MEI: And firms paid them anyway. That firms pay dividends at all while they carry a tax cost is the dividend puzzle.

MIGUEL: Clienteles.

MEI: The tax cost of a dividend differs across investors. In the US, stocks in retirement accounts, pension funds, and companies, which can deduct seventy percent, pay little or nothing on dividends. So a firm's payout policy attracts the investors it suits: the clientele effect. In 1996 individuals held fifty-four percent of US stocks but received only thirty-five percent of dividends.

MIGUEL: The Value Line figure.

MEI: Value Line announced a special dividend of seventeen dollars fifty a share in 2004. Three things in the figure. At the announcement, the price rose by about ten dollars: paying out cash the firm didn't need removed a tax cost and the risk of wasting it. On the ex-dividend date, the price fell by seventeen dollars ninety-one, close to the dividend, as section A predicts. And the volume jumped before the ex-date and faded afterwards: investors who pay no tax on dividends bought the share to collect it, then sold it back. The clientele effect in action, a strategy called dividend capture.

## Pay out or retain?

MEI: The first branch of the tree, in four cases. Case one, perfect markets, Example 17.4. Barston has a hundred thousand of excess cash. Pay it now, or invest it for a year in Treasury bills at six percent and pay it next year. Pay now: a hundred thousand today. Pay later: a hundred and six thousand in a year, worth a hundred thousand today. Same present value, and shareholders could have bought the bills themselves. MM payout irrelevance: in a perfect market, if excess cash is invested in financial securities, paying out or retaining doesn't affect firm value.

MIGUEL: Case two, corporate taxes.

MEI: Example 17.5. Same firm, but it pays thirty-five percent tax on the interest. After-tax return inside the firm: six percent times zero point six five, three point nine percent. Next year's payout: a hundred and three thousand nine hundred, less than the hundred and six thousand a tax-free investor would have earned alone. Interest received by the firm is taxed, just as interest paid saves tax. Cash is negative debt, so holding cash has a tax cost. A reason to pay out.

MIGUEL: Case three, the cost of raising capital.

MEI: If the firm pays out today and needs money tomorrow, going back to the market is expensive: issuance costs of about one to three percent for debt, three and a half to seven percent for equity. So firms keep cash when they expect large needs, research programmes, acquisitions, or volatile earnings. Cash is a financial cushion and keeps the firm away from distress. A trade-off, as for leverage: the tax cost of holding cash against the cost of raising it later.

MIGUEL: Case four, agency costs.

MEI: Cash beyond what the firm needs tempts managers: pet projects, perks, overpaying for acquisitions, and deep pockets that unions or regulators can reach for. Example 17.7. Rexton has a hundred and fifty million of cash, a hundred million shares, future free cash flows of sixty-five million a year, cost of capital ten percent. Management wants to spend the cash on an expansion that raises cash flows by twelve percent.

MIGUEL: Expand.

MEI: Sixty-five times one point one two, seventy-two point eight a year, worth seven hundred and twenty-eight million: seven dollars twenty-eight a share. Repurchase instead: business worth six hundred and fifty plus a hundred and fifty of cash, eight hundred million, eight dollars a share, and after buying back eighteen point seven five million shares, still eight dollars. The expansion costs a hundred and fifty and adds only seven point eight over zero point one, seventy-eight, of value: NPV minus seventy-two million. Paying out avoids a bad investment.

MIGUEL: So the payout isn't the value. Avoiding the bad project is.

MEI: Exactly. Payout policy matters here because it changes what managers do with the money, which is a violation of the "investment fixed" clause in MM.

## Smoothing and signalling

MEI: Back to GM, now with earnings and dividends per share on the same chart, 1985 to 2008. Earnings swing wildly, from above two dollars a share to below minus four, with deep losses in the early nineties and again from 2005. Dividends are a staircase: a few flat steps, sixty cents, seventy-five, forty, twenty, fifty for nearly a decade, then twenty-five. GM changed its dividend only eight times in twenty years while its earnings went from large profits to large losses. That's dividend smoothing. Firms try to pay regular dividends and to hold a constant payout ratio over time.

MIGUEL: Which makes a change a signal.

MEI: A policy of high dividends is costly if the finances aren't solid, so an increase signals that managers are confident about future cash flows. A decrease is a bad signal about that confidence. And for repurchases: managers can raise the value of the firm by buying back undervalued shares, so a repurchase is a positive signal that they think the share is cheap.

MIGUEL: What to remember.

MEI: Six lines. On the ex-dividend day the price falls by the dividend, and shareholders are no poorer. Dividend or repurchase: same firm value, same wealth, one lowers the price, the other the number of shares. MM dividend irrelevance, 1961: with investments fixed, in a perfect market the policy doesn't affect the share price, and shareholders can make homemade dividends. Taxes favour repurchases when dividends are taxed more, and clienteles differ. Retaining cash costs tax and invites waste but avoids the cost of raising capital later. And dividends are smoothed, so a change is a signal, and a repurchase signals that managers think the share is cheap.

## Wrap-up: the recap

MEI: Lecture ten in one minute. Four dates, and the ex-dividend date is the one that moves the price, by exactly the dividend in a perfect market. A dividend cuts the price and keeps the shares; a repurchase cuts the shares and keeps the price; equity is the same either way, nine hundred thousand in the first example, and a shareholder can convert one into the other by trading a few shares.

MIGUEL: Genron.

MEI: Forty-two dollars under all three policies: two now and four eighty after, nothing now and five oh four after, four fifty for ever. A higher dividend today means lower dividends later, same present value. The exercise: ten for ever or eleven for ever starting a year later, both a hundred today. MM 1961: with investments fixed, dividend policy is irrelevant; the bird in the hand can be caught by selling a share.

MIGUEL: In practice.

MEI: Dividends are taxed at once, repurchases let you choose, so taxes favour repurchases, and yet firms pay dividends: the dividend puzzle. Clienteles sort themselves: individuals held fifty-four percent of stocks and got thirty-five percent of dividends; Value Line's special dividend drew tax-free buyers who captured it and left. Retaining cash costs tax inside the firm and invites bad projects, Rexton's minus seventy-two million, but saves issuance costs of up to seven percent. Dividends are smoothed, GM changed its eight times in twenty years, so a cut is news and a buyback is a vote of confidence.

MIGUEL: The quiz.

MEI: Why does the price fall by the dividend and not more or less? Cum-dividend the share is worth the dividend plus the future ones, ex-dividend only the future ones; any other drop would be free money for one trade or the other. A firm with a million shares at twenty dollars and two million of spare cash: a dividend of two dollars gives a price of eighteen with a million shares; a repurchase buys a hundred thousand shares, leaving nine hundred thousand worth twenty each; wealth is twenty million in both cases. Can a dividend cut be good news? Usually a cut means lower expected earnings, but if the firm cuts to fund positive-NPV projects, it means more value later and the price can rise, as for Royal and SunAlliance in 2001.
