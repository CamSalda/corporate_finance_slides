## Recap of lecture four

MEI: Seven recap slides, and they're the whole CAPM in miniature. The Capital Market Line: everyone mixes the risk-free asset with the same risky portfolio, the tangency portfolio, and those mixes lie on one straight line whose slope is the Sharpe ratio of the tangency portfolio. And which portfolio sits at the tangency point? The market.

MIGUEL: Is a stock worth adding?

MEI: Only if its expected return pays for the risk it adds to your portfolio, and that risk depends on how the stock moves with what you already hold. The required return is the risk-free rate plus the beta times the portfolio's risk premium. Beta is the covariance of the two divided by the variance of the portfolio, or the correlation times the ratio of the volatilities. Intel and Coca-Cola: with zero correlation, beta zero, required return three percent, buy. With correlation one half, beta zero point two five, required return eight point seven five, don't.

MIGUEL: The assumptions.

MEI: Three. Everyone lends and borrows at the risk-free rate. Everyone shares the same estimates. Everyone holds an efficient portfolio. Strong assumptions, says the slide, in two words.

MIGUEL: Then the market.

MEI: With only Intel and Coca-Cola, everyone holds sixty-five point seven percent Intel and thirty-four point three Coca-Cola. Add up all investors, and sixty-five point seven percent of all the money in stocks is in Intel, so Intel is sixty-five point seven percent of the market. The market portfolio holds all risky assets at market-value weights, and in equilibrium it is the tangency portfolio.

MIGUEL: And the CAPM itself.

MEI: Replace the portfolio by the market in the required return. A stock that paid more than required would raise the market's Sharpe ratio, everyone would buy it, and the price would rise until the extra return disappears. A stock that paid less would be sold until the gap closes. So every stock pays the risk-free rate plus its beta times the market risk premium. Only market risk is paid. Example: four percent plus zero point three three times six percent gives six percent.

MIGUEL: And the Security Market Line.

MEI: The CAPM drawn with beta on the horizontal axis. Every stock and every portfolio sits on it. On the CML, with total risk on the axis, only mixes of the market and the risk-free asset do. Recap done.

## Introduction: Bill Miller

MEI: The transition slide sets up today in one sentence: lecture four said every stock sits on the Security Market Line, so nobody can beat the market. Today we ask whether that holds in practice. And the introduction opens with a story.

MIGUEL: Bill Miller.

MEI: Bill Miller, manager of the Legg Mason Value Trust fund. He beat the market every year from 1991 to 2005. Fifteen years in a row. Investors followed: the fund grew from zero point seven billion dollars to twenty-eight billion.

MIGUEL: And then.

MEI: And then 2007 and 2008. The fund lost almost sixty-five percent of its value, and with it all its lead over the market. At the end of 2008 it held one point two billion dollars.

MIGUEL: From twenty-eight billion to one point two.

MEI: The question on the slide: skill, or fifteen years of luck? The CAPM says nobody should beat the market persistently. Today we check that claim against how investors behave and what the data show.

MIGUEL: Outline.

MEI: Six sections, all from chapter 13. A, what does beating the market mean? B, who is on the other side of the trade? C, do investors behave as the CAPM says? D, does anyone actually win? E, strategies that seem to beat the CAPM. F, so, do we keep the CAPM?

## A. Beating the market: the alpha

MEI: Section A. The box says it: to beat the market you need stocks that pay more than the CAPM says they must. Competition between investors leaves few of them, and not for long.

MIGUEL: And the alpha is?

MEI: The one formula of the day. The alpha of a stock is what it is expected to pay, minus what the CAPM requires it to pay. Expected return minus required return, with the required return straight from lecture four. Positive alpha: the stock pays more than its risk requires, it sits above the Security Market Line. Negative alpha: below. And an alpha of zero for every stock is the CAPM itself, every stock on the line.

MIGUEL: Example.

MEI: Risk-free rate four percent, expected market return ten: market risk premium six percent. Stock X has a beta of one point two and an expected return of thirteen percent. Required return: four plus one point two times six, eleven point two percent. Alpha: thirteen minus eleven point two, plus one point eight percent. It pays more than its risk requires. Buy.

MIGUEL: Stock Y.

MEI: Beta zero point five, expected return six percent. Required return: four plus zero point five times six, seven percent. Alpha: six minus seven, minus one percent. Pays less than its risk requires. Sell. And the point the slide insists on: each stock is judged against its own required return, not against the other stock. Stock Y isn't bad because it returns less than X. It's bad because it returns less than a beta of zero point five deserves.

MIGUEL: And beating the market means?

MEI: Holding more of X and less of Y than the market does. That gives a higher Sharpe ratio than the market. That's the definition.

## When news arrives, and competition

MEI: Next, a picture. Start in the CAPM world: all alphas are zero, the market is the tangency portfolio. Then news arrives. Good for Walmart and Nike, expected returns up two percent. Bad for McDonald's and Tiffany, down two percent. Until prices move, alphas are not zero and the market portfolio is no longer the best one.

MIGUEL: So for a moment there's money on the table.

MEI: For a moment. Investors who see the news buy the positive-alpha stocks and sell the negative-alpha ones. And here's the mechanism, with numbers. Buying raises the price, which lowers the return. Stock X costs a hundred euros and should be worth a hundred and thirteen in a year: thirteen percent. Buyers push the price to about a hundred and one point six. From there, a hundred and thirteen is a return of eleven point two percent. The alpha is gone.

MIGUEL: So the alpha gets eaten by the people chasing it.

MEI: That's the box: competition between investors keeps alphas close to zero, and so keeps the market portfolio close to efficient. Notice what this means. The CAPM doesn't hold because investors are passive. It holds because they're greedy and fast.

## B. Every buyer needs a seller

MEI: Section B starts with a sentence that seems obvious and changes everything. Somebody sold stock X at a hundred euros. The buyer's gain is the seller's loss.

MIGUEL: Sure.

MEI: Now scale it up. All investors together hold the whole market. So the average investor earns the market return: an alpha of zero. For one investor to earn a positive alpha, another must earn a negative one. Alpha is a zero-sum game.

MIGUEL: So if I know nothing, I'm the loser.

MEI: That's the natural fear, and the slide answers it with a character. Meet Jérôme.

MIGUEL: A very French uninformed investor. At ESCP, in Paris, of course it's Jérôme.

MEI: Jérôme has no information about any stock. He knows that other investors have a great deal of information and use it when they build their portfolios. He worries that his portfolio will do worse than theirs. The question in the box: how can Jérôme guarantee that his portfolio does as well as that of the average investor?

MIGUEL: And the answer is beautifully lazy.

MEI: He holds the market portfolio. Every stock in proportion to its market value, as an index fund does. Why does it work? All investors together hold the market, so the market return is the return of the average investor. Jérôme earns it whatever the others know.

MIGUEL: And how would he lose?

MEI: By moving away from the market. If he holds less of a stock than the market does, informed investors hold more of it. When they're right about good news, they gain at his expense. The box: holding the market guarantees an alpha of zero. It needs no information and no skill.

MIGUEL: In practice, how do you hold the market?

MEI: Nobody can buy every risky asset in the world. So we approximate the market with a stock index: the S&P 500, the five hundred largest US firms; the CAC 40, the forty largest French firms; the Euro Stoxx 50; the MSCI World. And the simplest way to hold an index is an ETF, an exchange-traded fund: one share that holds all the stocks of the index, in its weights, at a low fee.

MIGUEL: Passive versus active.

MEI: Two boxes. Passive asset management: hold the index, through an ETF for instance, and don't try to pick stocks. You earn the market return minus a small fee. Active asset management: pick stocks or time the market, trying to beat the index. You aim for a positive alpha, but you pay higher fees and trading costs.

## When can the market be beaten?

MEI: Now the logical puzzle. If every uninformed investor held the market, like Jérôme, informed investors would have nobody to gain from. Beating the market needs many investors who do not hold the market. Why would they not?

MIGUEL: Two reasons on the slide.

MEI: One: they misread information. They believe they're earning a positive alpha when they're earning a negative one. Economists say they don't have rational expectations. Two: they care about something other than expected return and volatility, and they're willing to hold an inefficient portfolio for it. Either way, we need to look at how investors actually behave.

MIGUEL: And the slide "Who is on the other side?" spells out the argument in four steps.

MEI: Step one: anyone can get an alpha of zero for free, just hold the market. Step two: on average, investors earn an alpha of zero, because together they hold the market. Step three: so if you earn plus two percent of alpha, someone else earns minus two. When you bought the underpriced stock, someone sold it to you. Step four: who accepts minus two percent when zero is free?

MIGUEL: Nobody sensible.

MEI: Not an investor who behaves as the theory assumes. The box: to beat the market, you need a counterpart who behaves differently from what the theory predicts. And that's the door into behavioral finance.

MIGUEL: Define a bias.

MEI: A behavioral bias is a predictable mistake that many investors make. It pushes them away from holding the market, and costs them return. These biases are what put losers on the other side of the trade. The professor organizes them in two steps: what individual investors do, and whether their mistakes move prices.

## C. Mistake 1: too little diversification

MEI: The CAPM says everyone holds the market, thousands of stocks. In reality, among US households that own stocks, the median household holds four stocks. Four. Nine out of ten hold fewer than ten.

MIGUEL: Four stocks is not a portfolio, that's a shopping list.

MEI: And employees keep about a third of their retirement savings in the stock of their own employer. So they carry idiosyncratic risk, which earns nothing, lecture four. Why do they do it? Familiarity bias: people buy the companies they know. And keeping up with the Joneses: people care about doing as well as their neighbors, so they hold what their neighbors hold.

MIGUEL: Mistake two.

MEI: Too much trading. The CAPM says buy the market and hold it; there's little reason to trade. In reality, trading volume is enormous. Why? Overconfidence: investors believe they know more than the others. And sensation seeking: trading is exciting.

MIGUEL: I feel seen.

MEI: The table is the cure. Clients of a US discount broker, 1991 to 1997, sorted by how much they trade. The least active fifth earned about eighteen point five percent a year after costs. The most active fifth, about eleven point five percent. The market index, the S&P 500, about eighteen. The more they trade, the less they keep. Trading costs eat the return.

MIGUEL: Seven percentage points a year for the excitement.

MEI: Seven points a year. Expensive hobby.

## Do these mistakes move prices?

MEI: Now the careful question. Too little diversification and too much trading both contradict the CAPM. Does the rest of the CAPM fall with them? Not necessarily. If the mistakes are random, they cancel out. One investor holds too much Danone, another too little. Together they still hold the market, and prices don't move.

MIGUEL: So what matters is whether everyone makes the same mistake.

MEI: The box: individual mistakes change prices only if many investors make the same mistake at the same time, a systematic bias. And there are three on the slide.

MIGUEL: The disposition effect.

MEI: Selling winners too early and holding on to losers too long. Investors are about twice as likely to sell a stock at a gain as at a loss. And the losers they kept then did three point four percent worse over the next year than the winners they sold. They kept the wrong ones.

MIGUEL: Attention and mood.

MEI: Investors buy the stocks that are in the news, and buy more when they feel good. The number that always gets a laugh: in New York, the market's annualized return was about twenty-four point eight percent on sunny days against eight point seven on cloudy days.

MIGUEL: So the weather forecast is a trading signal. Professor Saldarriaga must love teaching this one on a rainy Paris morning.

MEI: It writes itself. Third: herd behavior. Investors imitate each other, because they think the others know something, or because they fear being left behind.

MIGUEL: And what do the biases imply?

MEI: They push many investors the same way, so they can push prices away from what the CAPM says. But only if two conditions hold: the biases are widespread and they last, and better informed investors cannot fully trade against them. Which leads to the box: if ordinary investors make systematic mistakes, better informed investors should be able to profit from them. Do they? Now the professionals.

## D. Trading on news: takeover offers

MEI: Section D. The simplest test of whether informed investors profit from others' mistakes: trade on public news, hoping others are slow to react. The news chosen is a takeover offer: firm A offers to buy all the shares of firm B, usually well above B's current price. Big news for B's shareholders.

MIGUEL: The example.

MEI: Marked "illustrative" on the slide, the professor being careful. B trades at forty euros. A offers sixty. The same day, B jumps to fifty-five, not sixty. Why not sixty?

MIGUEL: Because the deal may fail.

MEI: Because the deal may fail. If it succeeds, say a seventy-five percent chance, you get sixty: a gain of five from today's fifty-five. If it fails, twenty-five percent, B falls back to forty: a loss of fifteen. Expected price: zero point seven five times sixty plus zero point two five times forty equals fifty-five. Today's price. The five-euro gap is no free gain. It pays you for the risk that the deal fails.

MIGUEL: And the chart on the next slide?

MEI: It averages many takeovers, from a 1983 study. It shows B's abnormal return, the return above what its beta predicts, added up month after month. For all firms: a jump at month zero, then flat. Buying after the news earns no alpha. Split the sample: the firms actually taken over keep rising, the ones not taken over fall back. Main idea: public news is in the price the same day. To earn alpha you must know more than others. Here, predict whether the deal will succeed.

## Do fund managers have skill?

MEI: Fund managers are the best informed investors. The professor splits it into two questions, and the split is the whole point.

MIGUEL: Question one.

MEI: Do managers find extra value? Some do. Before fees, the average fund adds about three million dollars a year. But the median fund destroys value: most managers trade so much that their costs exceed what they find.

MIGUEL: Average positive, median negative. So a few very good ones pull the average up.

MEI: Exactly. Question two: do their investors receive it? No. After fees, the alpha of the average fund is about minus zero point three percent a year for its investors. Before fees is the gross alpha, after fees the net alpha, and the investor only earns the net alpha.

MIGUEL: Two pictures.

MEI: Left: larger funds add more value, the bars, yet their investors earn no more, the line. Right: managers beat the market before pension funds hire them, and are average after. From Berk and van Binsbergen, 2015, and Goyal and Wahal, 2008.

MIGUEL: Why don't investors capture the skill?

MEI: Money chases performance. When a manager does well, investors pour money into the fund: Bill Miller's went from zero point seven to twenty-eight billion. And a bigger fund is harder to run. Good ideas are limited and get spread over more money, and fees take the rest. Money keeps coming until investors can expect no more than the market return.

MIGUEL: So past winners don't keep winning.

MEI: Two numbers. Buying each year the funds on the Forbes honor roll, for nineteen years: eleven point two percent a year, against thirteen point one for an index fund. Pension funds hire managers who beat the market by about three point five percent a year over the previous three years. After hiring, they're average. And back to Bill Miller: the investors who arrived after fifteen good years got the two bad ones.

MIGUEL: Winners and losers, then.

MEI: The average investor earns an alpha of zero before trading costs. Doing better needs special skill or lower costs. Individual investors usually have neither: in Taiwan, individuals lose three point eight percent a year through their trading, institutions gain one point five. Skilled managers exist, but they keep the gain through their fees. The box: for most people the CAPM's advice is the best one. Hold the market, an index fund, mixed with the risk-free asset.

MIGUEL: Which is exactly what Jérôme does.

MEI: Jérôme wins again. One loose end, though: some simple strategies seem to beat the CAPM.

## E. Three patterns the CAPM misses

MEI: Section E. Three strategies that use only public information have earned positive alphas over long periods. Size: small firms, low market value, have earned more than their beta justifies. Value: the book-to-market ratio is the accounting value of a firm's equity divided by its market value, and stocks with a high ratio, value stocks, have earned more than stocks with a low ratio, growth stocks. Momentum: stocks that rose the most over the last six to twelve months keep doing better, for a few months, than those that fell the most. Buying the winners and selling the losers earned an alpha above twelve percent a year in the US, 1965 to 1989.

MIGUEL: The evidence.

MEI: Two charts from Ken French's data library, 1926 to 2015. Each dot is a group of stocks sorted by size on the left, by book-to-market on the right. Under the CAPM every dot would be on the Security Market Line. Most are above it.

MIGUEL: Real, or found by chance?

MEI: The professor raises data snooping. Test enough characteristics and one will match past returns by pure luck. The famous joke result: butter production in Bangladesh "predicted" the US stock market.

MIGUEL: I would like to see that regression.

MEI: It exists, and that's the warning. But size, value and momentum are harder to dismiss: they show up in other periods and other countries. And there's a logic to size and value. If a stock has a positive alpha, for whatever reason, its price is low compared with what it pays. A low price means a low market value and a high book-to-market ratio. So if the CAPM misses something, small stocks and value stocks are exactly where the positive alphas should appear.

MIGUEL: What could explain the patterns?

MEI: A positive-alpha strategy everyone knows should be competed away, and these have been known for decades and are still there. The likelier explanation, in the box: the market portfolio we use is not exactly efficient, so a stock's beta with it misses part of the stock's risk. Three reasons. Proxy error: the true market holds every asset, property, private firms, bonds, and a stock index is only a stand-in. Behavioral biases: the mistakes of section C lead investors to hold inefficient portfolios. And other risks investors care about: someone who works in a bank already depends on banks for a salary, and may avoid bank stocks on purpose.

MIGUEL: And the fix is more factors.

MEI: If the beta with the market misses part of the risk, measure risk against several portfolios instead of one. The best-known version uses four: the market; small firms minus big firms; value stocks minus growth stocks; past winners minus past losers. A stock gets one beta for each, and its required return is the risk-free rate plus each beta times the premium of that portfolio. The CAPM logic with four terms instead of one. Example: McDonald's cost of capital, about seven point one percent with the CAPM, about eight point two with the four factors.

## F. What firms use in practice

MEI: Section F, the reality check. A survey of 392 chief financial officers asked how they estimate the cost of capital, several answers allowed. CAPM: seventy-three point five percent. Historical average returns: forty percent. A model with several factors: about one third. Dividend discount model: sixteen percent.

MIGUEL: So after a whole lecture on its problems, the CAPM wins anyway.

MEI: Why it stays: it's simple and well founded, and the discount rate is only one imprecise input. Errors in the cash flows often matter more. The box: the CAPM is imperfect, and still the standard. We use it for the cost of capital in lecture six. Which, by the way, is the next episode.

MIGUEL: How it all connects.

MEI: Six points. Beating the market means holding positive-alpha stocks. Competition erases alphas, and every buyer needs a seller: the average investor earns zero alpha. Ordinary investors make mistakes, and some are shared by many. Professionals find some value, but fees and inflows take it. Size, value and momentum have beaten the CAPM, so the market portfolio is probably not exactly efficient. And the CAPM stays the standard: simple, well founded, close enough.

## Wrap-up: the recap

MEI: Lecture five in one minute. Alpha is expected return minus required return, and under the CAPM it's zero for every stock. Competition erases alphas: buying pushes the price up and the return down. Every buyer needs a seller, so the average investor earns zero alpha, and every positive alpha is someone else's negative one. Jérôme holds the market and gets zero alpha with no information and no skill. To beat the market you need a counterpart who behaves differently from the theory.

MIGUEL: The behavior.

MEI: Individuals hold four stocks and trade too much: the most active fifth earned seven points a year less than the least active. Random mistakes cancel; systematic ones, the disposition effect, attention and mood, herding, can move prices. Public news is in the price the same day, so the takeover gap is a risk premium, not a free lunch. Fund managers find some value on average but the median destroys it, and after fees investors get minus zero point three percent. Money chases performance until the alpha is gone.

MIGUEL: The patterns.

MEI: Size, value and momentum beat the CAPM over long periods. Probably because the index is an imperfect stand-in for the true market portfolio. Multifactor models add more betas. And seventy-three point five percent of CFOs still use the CAPM.

MIGUEL: The quiz.

MEI: Investors buy a stock with a positive alpha: the price rises, the expected return falls, until the alpha is back to zero. Uninformed investors guarantee a non-negative alpha by holding the market portfolio. High trading volume is inconsistent with the CAPM because in equilibrium everyone holds the market and only adjusts the mix with the risk-free asset, so there's little reason to trade; heavy trading means disagreement and overconfidence. And the systematic biases: the disposition effect, attention and mood, and herd behavior.

MIGUEL: My takeaway: the cheapest investment advice in the world is "be Jérôme." Hold the index, don't check the weather, and let the overconfident traders pay for your lunch.
